Questioning Consciousness, and being too American for Communism

Hello friends and family,

I’ve spent all weekend out on the deck overlooking the lagoon. On this wooden deck, I am either: drying off after a swim, calling friends from home, reading, and chatting with people from the program, or having my morning coffee, often with Emerson. A few moments ago there were three little boys on a kyak from one of the closeby homes. I heard them chanting in unison, and before I could wonder what they were saying, the kyak flipped over. Once they resurfaced, a choir of giggling ensued. They are most certainely at the home next door where a big family gathering is taking place– which most families on the island have on Sunday after church. People wake up around 4:30am and go to the market and their fisherman before church, and then have a big party for the rest of the day. All shops and stands are closed on the island around 9am. They know how to spend the Sabbath right. The Mormon church has a large presence on the island, but instead of the stories being about Americans, apparently they are about Polynesians, and they sing Thaitian songs during service, not America. Our assistant director for the program is a Mormon, and the single student in our program who speaks French went to church with her. I wish I were a Polyglot so I could go and listen to what the hell is going on (curiously and respectfully).

This last week our class mostly consisted of us snorkeling, and one of our activites was a scavenger hunt identify and take a photo of 100 different species that could be in the reef, and we were broken up into five different teams. Our team only had two cameras, so being the third snorkeler I was quite unhelpful to my teammates. But it turned into a lovely hour and a half in the water. We got seperated to try and cover as much ground as possible, and I found myself completely alone among the coral. It was marvelous. The section I was in was a part of the reef I have spent little time in. I felt exactly like I was solo traveling in a new place, and the large coral structures reminded me of tall buildings, with the different fish as residents. My imagintation was entirely inspired by Ariel from that Disney movie, and soon my surroundings underwater felt like the most vibrant city I had visited. It reminded me of some of my favorite parts of traveling alone: I could go completely at my own pace, and none of my brain capactiy was spent making sure my companion was okay and also enjoying themselves. Oftentimes we move quite quickly through the reef, leaving less time to explore small crevases. The quick movement is not necessarily intentional, but is encouraged by the current pushing us along. The current was another glorious part about this particular time in the water, I was close enough to the reef break that it pushed me along with the other fish. When I stopped resisting I was being guided through the thick, vibrant, coral city. A group of different juvenile fish species were trying to eat the coral, but since the coral was mostly white, it meant that the baby fish were at best, practicing eating. I felt like I was seeing them at school, learning how to eat, as well as hide from predators, which they did very well as they swam deep into the coral once I got too close. It also reminded me of my lesser enjoyable parts of traveling to a new place with little time: I often feel out of place, intruding, but also quite invisible. Here, the same is true, and the same is true the fish and other underwater inhibitors (rather than people) are going about there buisness as usual. I cannot particpate, but I can observe.

I read Mark Fisher’s book “Capitalist Realism” yesterady, which I don’t entirely agree with (I think I am too American: entrenched in my liberal ideas that I assume are natural). But he brought up this interesting concept of the “cancellation of the longterm” of our society, especially in regards to our media consumption. I found the phrase entirely provocative, and realizing now that it couples very well with Calvino’s memos, and his belief that “reading an image” is entirely necessary, but we struggle because we have become over saturated. So maybe we are loosing our ability to read an image is the causation for the cancellation of the longterm, because what will happen to us once we completely lose the practice? I have not interrogated this connection enough, will work on that and get back to you.

I also think of a recent NYT interview I read with a man named Michael Pollan, who has sparked my curioisty and also skeptism. But that is besides the point. The point is that he discussed how humanity doesn’t necessarily abid by more humane rules if a being has consciousness, animals being the most poignant example. I don’t know if this was the Buhddism in my upbringing or something else, but I thought we all knew that animals have consciousness on some level? But maybe we don’t know that for certian. This made me think further: does something such as coral have consciousness? When they release their eggs and sperm after a full moon (meaning they have a perdictable cycle), does that require some sort of consciousness? And then what do we even mean by consciousness? Is anything able to create life, conscious? Is the fact that trees might be able to communicate with each other mean they are beings? I think yes, but I am sure that there is much to read about on this subject. All I know is that I know nothing at all. I look forward to reading more Pollan. There was an excellent photo of him and his wife on a hike, and I felt like I could see them as a couple in their twenties, which brought a smile to my face. Not sure what I mean by this, but maybe you will find the photo and I hope you can see it too.

I’ve also been reading “Song of the Dodo” alongside my other books. It has been wonderful to read about different adventures people went on, in an attempt to explain the world. I will end this bamboozle of writing with a quote that put a big smile on my face. The author writes about a trip he took to an island in Indonesia, “I’ve begun to like this guy for his earnestness, but still I wish he would lighten up. Feeding dead goats to Komodo dragons for the edification of the sun-addled tourists could be construed, after all, as a sick sort of fun. There are worse jobs in the world than his.” The world, and humans, are so so weird. May you have some weirdness in your days.

Rebekah
