Grounded in Music & Jumping into Eel Infested Waters

Hello friends and family,

After a good sit down “staring off into the distance” session with me myself and I, I am happy to report that I feel much better. My mind felt quite hazy the last few days, with a mixture of sadness and stiffness, which is a feeling I am always afraid will never end, but it always does. But luckily the haze that was altering my vision has lifted. I don’t know if it was something in particular, it could have been intensified after having calls with my mom over the past week, each time having the same conversation, since she seems to forget each time we have spoken. It could have also had to do with some social dynamics that remind me of high school, which has compelled me to spend more time reading Isaiah Berlin and “A Place of My Own” by Michael Pollan in my hammock. Or rather, I should flip that on its head and say that reading Berlin and Pollan in my hammock is oftentimes simply too enticing to pass up. I’m grateful to learn that when my hierarchy of needs is met, I am content reading in a hammock staring out into the distance with good tunes playing. Although I would be lying if I feel totally content…

Not to say that I’ve been a total recluse. Before I begin my writings about the other night and the following morning I would like structure these stories by songs. For the first song, we are brought to LCD Soundsystem’s “I can change” Last night I sat with Emerson on the dock for his cigarette break and we had lovely conversations about our fears and God as we stared up at the stars. We concluded with LCD Soundsystem before we returned to the group so we could all go out to the bar.

I cannot provide any song to accompany us to the bar, because there was live Tahitian music that I do not know. But for shits and giggles let’s go with “Stuck in the Middle With You” for no good reason, but just because I can! Not many details here that I can share with such a broad audience. But all you need to know is that it was fun.

Once we were– I will say encouraged to leave– we made our short walk back to Gump Station and soon all of us jumped into the water. It was almost too perfect to be true, but the song accompanying this portion of the night was “Harvest Moon” by Neil Dimond. I remember the music being very loud, so I think someone must have brought out the loud speaker. It was absolutely lovely swimming in the warm waters, floating on our backs staring at the starry night. We were all giggling and trying to stay far apart from one another since we were all skinny dipping. The outline of the mountains were beautiful even though they were lacking any color. I find that in my time here I feel most like a little kid when I am in the water, twisting and turning, diving down for no particular reason, weightless in the saltwater as it keeps me afloat. I spent some time on the dock once we all got out and had existential conversations about climate change and the future. I decided it was time to go inside, and join “gay girl support group” which I qualify for given that (and I quote, not my words) I am “culturally gay”. I took a lovely shower and crawled into bed.

I must include the morning because I quickly got onto my bike– which is a cruiser with no gears (or only one gear?), rusted bike handles, purple, and a lot of personality. I hit shuffle on my spotify playlist that I’ve been compiling on this trip:“i was motioning” (if you have me on Spotify), and I headed on my way. I set out with no cell phone service and no plan, only the day ahead of me. The first song that came up was “Yes I’m Changing” by Tame Impala – who I don’t usually listen to, but very much fits the “vibe”. Soon following was “Little Mascara” by The Replacements . Every mode of moving: walking, driving, jet ski, boat, has its own impact on the way I perceive the environment around me. Something about being on a bike, by myself, along the one laned road with a non-existent shoulder, looking up at the variety of mountains to my left with the glittering water to my right, made the sights I was seeing sink in deeper, and I smiled and thought to myself, “holy shit, where am I?”. I turned back once I got to a public beach and I felt satisfied by my mini exploration. Once I returned to Gump I jumped into the water and went about my day, called friends from home, returned to the hammock, and enjoyed a restful Sunday.

A few days later…

Yesterday was Tuesday February 24th, and by 1pm I realized I was having one of the best days I’ve had in my life so far. I say that cautiously, I never like to make such absolutest claims about time and experiences, I’ve had many wonderful days within my ordinary life that are just as fulfilling overtime. But I evoke such wording because it was indeed so special. We got up at 5am (why do so many of my favorite memorable days begin with an early wakeup…), took the 7am ferry to Tahiti and got on a bus for a two hour drive to the end of the road on the remote side of Tahiti. For the last thirty minutes of the drive, our Professor sat next to me to get out of his sunny spot, and we exchanged our music. He was a radio DJ at Amherst with the afternoon Thursday/Friday slot, so I knew he would have the good cuts. He recommended, Brian Jonestown Massacre and Big Audio Dynamite In return, I shared with him that I was listening to, “I Will Dare” and explained that I try to play a particular album or artist when I am in a new place so whenever I listen, I will think of that place. For this trip, I have been listening to The Replacements. This is true, I’m not trying to be a pretentious asshole, although I know I am probably incredibly insufferable by now. But it feels authentic (but what is true authenticity, you may ask? I do not disagree). I thought of The Replacements because Emerson shared his earbuds with me the first week and we listened to “Swinging Party” on the ferry ride back to Mo’orea and it felt strange and really lovely listening to Minnesota grunge from the 80s on an island. Good to be strange!

Once our road ended we got onto a boat, and were informed that the elder we were supposed to meet, Papa Patrick, was not going to be with us. Bummer! We began our journey into the Rāhui, which were Tahitian moratoriums for fishing particular lagoons or portions of the water implemented by the Chiefs, if broken were punishable by death. Sometimes it was put in place after a low fishing season, or for a particular fish but not others, and other times was for mourning of a Chief or High Figure. They stopped being implemented in the 19th-20th centuries due to colonization, but are being brought back within the last decade by community members due to the fish yields being smaller and smaller each year. But since the Chiefs are the only ones who can implement a Rāhui, and there are no more Chiefs, the descendants and community members make a collective decision to implement them.

As we go further and further away from the last of the road, we still see houses– which are a part of a fishing community who get around by boat or walking, with each home having their own dock. This used to be the most populated part of the island, but now it is sparsely populated. The valleys were so deep there was no room for roads, and you begin to see how isolated their life truly is– but also how connected they are to the water, and to each other. Off in the distance I see an old man standing at the edge of a dock and think to myself “no way”. But yes way Rebekah, yes way. That is Papa Patrick– getting picked up in the most badass way. It turns out that he forgot we were coming and so we just picked him up outside of a house where he had been trying to persuade the family to vote for a local politician. Soon we arrive to our first destination, where we are supposed to go on a walk to a sacred stone. But we soon run into a slight problem… the cement dock had collapsed after hurricane season, but we maneuvered our way around. Our walk to the sacred stone was beautiful deep lush jungle with vines and mud surrounding us, but soon the water was bluntly before our side, clear and glistening.. It reminded me of being fourteen on Hog Island and doing the walk through the forest by myself. I often think of this as the moment I consciously fell in love with nature, and the world. There was thick moss here too, just like in Maine. I am always faced with a particular conundrum when hiking: where to look? If I look up and admire the beauty before me, I will be humbly reminded that I must look down where my feet are, to make sure that I do not trip or fall. I have not figured this out yet. Let me know if you have any tips or tricks.

The sacred rock was a flat six foot rock that I would have passed on a walk, but luckily Papa Patrick was there to tell us about it. Only people from his family can tell the story of the rock (thus his importance of being with us), and is also why I will not be sharing. But he told us to share what we learn here with our own communities and friends, because they are concerned about putting food on the table for their children. He said that the Rāhui was put into place as a way of savings, he said that we may think of savings for our children as dollars in a bank, but for him and his community the Rāhui is saving the fish for their children in the future. Out of all of the “your generation is the one that can save us!” speeches (which I find to be an eyeroll, given many people preaching that sentiment is maybe fifty at best), I took Papa Patrick’s the most seriously. He reminded me of Linda Hamiliton from ANCA in Redlands, although she does not have generations before her in Redlands, she is still an important community figure that people listen to, like Papa Patrick. It is lovely to visit new places and be able to see familiar faces within people's spirits.

Afterwards we got into the water, and again, I know I say this a lot, but it was striking. Strikingly beautiful, dramatic, and unique, the coral was on an underwater cliff. I can’t describe it any other way. There was deep blue water that you could not see the bottom of, in the same line of sight as lushes coral on a slope, with varieties of fish hiding within and throughout. I was awestruck. I would dive down as deep as I could to allow the coral to come into focus, look up, and realize I would have to get back up too, when I was at a depth of what felt like a two storied building beneath the surface (probably only the bottom of a room, but let’s use our imagination). It was a fun, but rushed, elevator back up to the surface.

We then got back onto the boat to go to a nearby eel-infested freshwater river with a supposed rope swing. The saltwater sank to the bottom, meaning your feet were warm and the cold freshwater met your waist. It turned out the rope swing was true, but we all disregarded it and we all began to jump from a dubious branch into a spotlight that the sun created through the shadowy trees. It was a bit of a rockclimb up the trunk to get to the dubious branch, but I was very happy I did because I have no words other than, it was super fun. We only had thirty minutes, and as I snorkeled back to the boat, I saw saltwater fish swimming underneath the freshwater fish above them. The water was blurry with the mixing of the waters, giving it an ominous feeling. Luckily we didn’t see any eels, although they were surely lurking.

Now that I’ve discussed the fun parts, I am going to devote some time to describing the scenery. I don’t believe photos are able to capture even 40% of the beauty that was before us. The mountains were humongous and abrupt, falling to the ground in unforgiving forms, covered in every shade of green. The valleys were neverending, the further off into the shape of the island you looked, the more valleys appeared. And the more jaggedly shaped mountains appeared, framed by two mountains. Waterfalls and stone ridges, with caves if you looked close enough. If you were hiking along the ridge and fell, it would not even be a tumble down the side: it would be a freefall. If you look away from the island you see clouds low in the horizon, almost meeting the sea. They were big and full, and wispy and light. The palm trees were hanging over the edge of the water almost meeting the lagoon, poking out through the curtain the trees created between the water and the shore.

The bus ride back, I was so incredibly happy I put on As She Was by the Talking Headsto capture the essence of what I was feeling. So incredibly happy. But I woke up this morning feeling entirely sad after a bad dream, and I am left with feeling like a cloud. Sometimes clouds are large, dark and heavy, other times they are large, bright and abundant, or they are spaced out within the blue skies: fast moving and light. But they all eventually pass, so I feel quite seen by the clouds above me. Another way of saying this is that I am a big ball of emotions. This would be a more simple and less peculiar antidote, choose your pick.

I am very grateful for you reading my thoughts and experiences, and whether you are in a place that is experiencing seasons, or not, I hope that you can feel a little bit like a cloud too, in whatever way that means to you. I recognize how I sound, but it is a bit fun to lean into it, no?

Talk soon!

Rebekah
