Rebekah’s Guide to Amsterdam

My first summer here, I experienced it largely as a tourist, getting my bearings and wandering. The second summer I felt in between; I was no longer a tourist but I was not a local either. This summer, I feel as close to a local as I could be without having permanent residence here. I have a yoga studio I go to now, (who is she?), and have made wonderful friends over the years, and I’ve been able to see the city through their eyes, as well as my own. And plus, I’ve biked through morning commute traffic; needless to say achieving that has given me a true sense of audacity.

I’m writing this guide from the perspective of what I would want to know based on my own experiences as a traveler, visiting somewhere for too short a time, and wishing I had a bit of insider information on which neighborhoods to see. I've learned that having a few references enable me to have the best experience to flânerie around a new city. I’ve been lucky enough to travel quite a bit over these summers, so I have developed a particular way of traveling which will show through this guide, if my way of traveling does not suite, please bend these suggestions to your will. I hope there is enough to be helpful but allow enough space for your own hopes and desires for the city to be met. This was not written as a checklist guide, I don't think you could feasibly do all of this in a few days. All I hope is that you find a love of Amsterdam!

How to get around:

Baseline is that there is very rarely any reason to take any form of car transportation. Totally understandable if public transportation is not something you are accustomed to, but the Netherlands is a great place to start. A few things to know though.

  • 1. Tap the machines to check in and check out, either with a ticket you bought at the airport (you can get a three day pass), a QR code, or most convenient, and I believe the same price: using your credit card (physical or your phone). Make sure you tap out as you leave or else they will charge you for until the end of the line.

  • 2. For trams going to more touristy places, the exits and entrances are clearly marked. You enter the tram where the two train conductors are located, and exit at the back or middle of the tram. This will be confusing at first, but after a few rides it will become obvious and in no time you will help your fellow visitor by pressing the green button to open the doors. The exit and entrance headache is disregarded if you are packed in the tram like a sardine.

    • From Schipol airport take the metro to Centraal Station; or depending on where you are staying, taking a bus might get you there quicker.

    A main way locals get around the Netherlands is via bike. I do not recommend you do this if you are here for a few days, especially if you are not used to riding a bike often. The only case where this makes sense to do is: if you often commute on your bike in a city, and or are an arrogant man. I think most accidents happen with tourists, and it takes me a week or two every year to loosen my grip on the handle bars and become accustomed. However, it is one of the only ways to be really a part of Dutch culture while being in this incredibly international city. If you do decide to do this, my best advice is to be decisive, respectful, and aware. Keep to the right, left is for passing. Make sure your tires do not run parallel to the train tracks.

    This city is also very walkable, and depending where you are staying, you will surely be able to do many activities by foot.

    If you are freshly coming from the United States and suffering with the rough jet lag, I recommend taking advantage of the early rising and go out for a stroll before tourists emerge, and see the centre alongside the morning commuters, and maybe even appreciate the way the light reflects off the canal. Same can be said for night here, it is incredibly safe, female solo travelers no need to fret, walking around the canals late at night surely has a special ambiance to it.

    Where to go:

    Here are my recommendations for someone eager to get out of the streams of tourists. If that is your jam, start at Valeriusplein and head towards the Dam, hit up the flowermarket, and stumble across the many coffeeshops that are at your disposal. My parents always used to say that there is no one a mile down the trail– and it turns out that phrase applies for cities, not just National Parks! Here are some things I have picked up that might be helpful to you if you want to do something different but might not know where to start:

  • The Jordaan neighborhood is a lovely way to see the canal in central Amsterdam without the crowds. Head to Westerpark and hopefully soak in the sun; and check out Cafe Checkpoint Charlie if you want a drink.

  • Head down to Amsterdam Oud-West and go to Foodhallen , extra incentive if you are there on a Saturday: there is a lively outdoor market full of fresh vegetables and used items, from belts, hats, and phones. Inside you have a great foodhall, and good local artisan shops that have some good souvenirs to bring home for friends and family. This area used to be an immigrant neighborhood, and the variety of cuisines is still evident. A great neighborhood to wander into vintage stores and cafes.

  • If you are in this direction and head towards the center, you should go to Waterkant, a great outdoor bar full of locals and is right on the canal. At the end of a sunny day it will be packed, but a great scene and lots of people will be rolling by on their boats as well as their bikes.

  • There are great English bookstore next to public housing for single women after the age of 35, although I can’t find it on Google Maps so you will have to find it. Although busy and deep in the center, very much worth seeing. A few steps away is a beautiful overgrown train track with grass and flowers in bloom if you are here during the summer months. Very #Pintrest. Best to eat at Fou Fow Ramen before taking the dip into the deep end.

  • My favorite street to window shop is Gasthuismolensteeg: start at Singel and make your way down. I hope you find your own favorite street to wander and window shop, but maybe this might be a nice place to start.

  • The flowermarket might be nice, and there is a restaurant Blue Amsterdam above the market.

  • If you are at Amsterdam Centraal, or have some time to kill before your train, go to OBA Oosterdok , a library with a nice cafe inside. Free wifi and a cool space.

  • De Balie is a wonderful restaurant, cafe, bar, with academic talks, performances. A favorite of mine.

  • Go on a canal tour. Although obviously touristy, it is a great way to see the city from the canals, pleasant, and you learn quite a bit! You can find one along the canal near Museumplein.

  • Last but not least, I think wandering around and finding a bar along a canal is the best way to go.

    • Museums:

    Of course I have many opinions on museums, but here are a few to check out while you are here. They are quite expensive (upwards of 20 euros), so look online and choose your favorites. If you are here for quite some time and are a real museum buff, getting a yearlong Museum card is worth it after about four visits. Beware of the inevitable Museum fatigue.

  • H’ART Museum: great neighborhood (best for houseboat admiring), and a great orientation for Dutch culture, with lots of art and great exhibits. There is a lovely cafe as well where you can soak in all of the knowledge you’ve just absorbed.

  • Although I have many disagreements with Donna Horn, “People love Dead Jews” has a great analysis of Anne Frank Huis, and so I have avoided the museum. Understandable to want to visit and I’m not in any way saying to change your arrangements if you already have it booked, but I recommend going to the Jood’s Museum in the East. It’s a beautiful neighborhood, and each year the museum has a great exhibit, and gives a very important background of Jews in the Netherlands, their contributions to Dutch society, and opportunity to hear the voices of those who survived, and stories of those who did not.

  • If you want an especially peculiar experience, the Art Zoo Museum was a wonderful surprise, It is a taxidermy museum, where you are in the cage rather than the animals (no impending sense of danger other than existential dread). Housed in an old Amsterdam mansion, it is beautiful and absurd. Great cafe and backyard garden, located straight in the city.

  • FOAM: is a modern photography museum, and as I write this, I am starting to recall that I really disliked it. You should check it out and see for yourself. Maybe I am full of shit, or the museum is. It’s a real toss up! Please report back.

  • Museum of Canales: I’ve never made it there, but I’ve always heard it is a must see.

    • Museumplein:

    Thankfully Dutch reads a lot like English (or English reads a lot like Dutch), and so you probably can guess that this is a stretch of Museums. There is a beautiful long open lawn, a good place to take a rest– with an Albert Heijn nearby in case you forgot to eat and want something quick and cheap.

  • The Van Gough: tickets sell out, so best to book your ticket before you arrive. The museum does a good job in creating an interesting narrative about Van Gogh and his brother, and has a few of his famous pieces, although the Rijks does as well. There are other pieces from people inspired by Van Gogh and his contemporaries. I would suggest going early to avoid the crowds, but to be honest I do not gravitate towards it very often and am only recalling the people hoarding the paintings as I write this.

  • The Rijksmuseum: My second summer here I spent hours here, mostly people watching. I have a favorite room: The Impressionist painters, look for the Gallery 1.18 on the 1700-1800 floor. I don’t think any other floors are worth it, but of course this is very subjective. My general advice is that this museum is simply too big to see it all and be strategic. Also very busy, and requires buying a ticket beforehand, although usually you can get a same day ticket; less cutthroat than Van Gogh (1.18 has some Van Gogh’s).

  • Stedelijk Museum: is the least crowded of the three, and has interesting modernist pieces.

    • Parks

    I live near Vondelpark, so it is the one I frequent the most, it is big and beautiful. My favorite place to go is a portion I call “The Stumbling Spot”, located down a side path, just to the west side of Groot Melkhuis if you are approaching from Zuid. It is along a pond and is always frequented by ducks and geese, and sometimes there is a man in a striped t-shirt playing the clarinet while sitting under a tree. There are many magical sections of the park like this, hopefully you can discover your own stumbling spot. The Netherlands, but especially Vondelpark, has a particular way of shining just after it has rained and the sun begins to peer its way out through the clouds. The leaves are shiny from the rain, the light is especially crisp, the birds are loud, and the Dutch are en masse soaking up their opportunity for Vitamin D. A sunny day and the park is covered with equal parts people and bikes on the lawn. A really excellent place to hang out.

  • More practical and helpful: Groot Melkhuis is a great spot for a bite, a drink, or dinner. A sunny day and this is the prime location. Teahouse Rhijnauwen is another bar option, I prefer the food at Groot but I think the atmosphere at Teehuis is probably best for a late night drink. Check out if there is free music at Vondelpark for your dates here.

  • De Pijp is the cool “hip” part of the city, and they have a great neighborhood park: Sarphatipark . A lot like Vondelpark, just smaller.

  • Het Amsterdamse Bos is out of the way but beautiful if you have been travelling a lot and are missing some green.

    • Amsterdam Noord:

    Go to Amsterdam Centraal and hop on one of the very short ferry ride to take you to Amsterdam Noord, it is free and serves as a bridge. If it is the last Saturday of the month, you should definitely go to see the largest Flea Market in Europe. Order tickets online and enjoy the abundance of options and ethical(?) consumerism. After grab a bite to eat at Pllek, or a restaurant like it along the coast. If you are there late and want to check it out, head to Skatecafe or Garage Noord for a party.

    Swimming:

    Especially hot days might entice you to jump in cold water, given the minimal option for AC. You can go to the Eastern Docklands, which is a residential neighborhood far out in Amsterdam.

  • There is swimming in Oosterdok, , and on the other side of the city at Park Schinkeleilanden, .

    • Live Music:

  • I’ve been enjoying going to BIMHUIS, to see fantastic jazz shows. I find Dutch crowds to be really weird and proper during shows. They often don’t sing along, and are just very polite. It turns out that it is well suited for jazz. Beautiful building, with a restaurant and bar, you can even bring your drinks in!

  • Paradiso, is a great concert venue, and is a converted old church. Check out who is playing while you are here!

  • Across the street is Melkweg, , another concert venue. Not as beautiful as Paradiso, but has smaller, more intimate concerts.

    • Practical Help for Moments in a Pinch:

  • Albert Hejin and Jumbo are your grocery stores, and have good prepackaged meals. It is very lovely to sit by a canal and just eat something from here (especially if you are a college student and your standards are low).

  • HEMA is akin to a Target from home. Did you forget an adaptor, a charger, or want to buy a candle? This is the place.

  • Etos is the drugstore here, and are throughout the city. Note, that pharmacies function like the intermediary of a CVS– able to make their own prescriptions and give you some crutches but not the emergency room. Great if you have a UTI or a nasty cold. The stereotype of Dutch healthcare is that they are generally unsympathetic to pain, a girl told me she fractured her skull and they told her she was fine. So you got to milk it. They don’t hand out morphine like they do in the States.

    • Other parts of the Netherlands:

    The train system enables you to get around the Netherlands very easily. The tickets have become more expensive (to the South it is about 40 euros), but if you have the budget for it I think it is worth it.

  • My favorite is Den Haag, they have beautiful beaches, and gardens.

  • Rotterdam is a port city, with great museums (check out the migration museum, only 7 euros).

  • Utrecht is closer, only a 20 minute train ride and a student city.

  • Delft and Liden are small yet beautiful, looking very familiar to Amsterdam, but very charming. Delft has good markets on Saturday.

  • The fishing and Dutch destination of Edam might be a nice change of pace. You are much more likely to be in a sea of Dutch compared to Amsterdam, so if you are itching to experience Dutch culture (but don’t want to go to Belgium), go here!

  • Amsterdam is, I think, about 40% international, and so English is primarily the default– especially if you are not tall and blonde. Generally the Dutch speak better English than we do, but if you are in smaller towns it will quickly reveal to you that you should have learned a few more words in Dutch. Danke goes a long way. Hello is Hello.

    • In summary:

  • Avoid cars and take public transportation

  • Museums are expensive, choose one or two. I say H’ART and Joods. Or swap out Joods for Rijks.

  • Neighborhoods that are nice alternatives to the center: Amsterdam Oud-West, Jordaan, Roeterseiland, De Pijp.

  • Go to the parks.

  • Go on a canal tour.

  • Get out early if you are awake.

  • Wander in order to stumble upon something ordinarily spectacular.

  • Take advantage of the cosmopolitan city and try and see some live music.

    • Safe travels and I hope this helps!

    Rebekah
