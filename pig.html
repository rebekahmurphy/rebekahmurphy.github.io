A Sunrise, a Pig, and Bryan from Glendale

Hello friends and family!

Although this last week was busy, it was in ways I was not expecting, classic life! On Wednesday we all woke up at 4:50am for our 5:45 departure for Tetiaroa via Catamaran. That night, I had been woken up maybe four times by the storm of harsh thunder, thick rain, and bright lightning that shined bright enough to see the light through my closed eyes. I had joined reality into my dream where our professor called off our trip to Tetiaroa. When I woke up it turned out the trip had not been cancelled, much to my surprise. I remembered Hinano Murphy’s reaction the day before when we told her we were taking a boat out, which was met with surprise but mostly disapproval. I put that together with the storm from last night and prepared myself for a bumpy ride. I had a light breakfast with two dramamines and went along with the morning, knowing the boat company would not put us in any danger. At 5:44I was just about done putting on my sunscreen and closing up my bag, when I walked out the door and Ted (my professor’s husband, who has a real dad sense of humor) immediately looked at me and said “we’re not going”. I had truly thought he was joking, because it was the exact time we were supposed to leave, and I had spent the last hour hyping myself up about our trip– mostly to convince myself that I was not going to have an ounce of seasickness. Turns out, Ted was not joking, he corrected me that since he is a grandpa now, he is very serious and his dad jokes have retired.

We all went out to the dock for a beautiful sunrise, with a variety of clouds and colors, it was quite nice. Because of our cancellation it meant that our final presentations were going to be in a few hours at 8:30 instead of the next day, but the double dose of dramamine meant that I needed to take a much needed nap after the sunrise.

After our final and class ended, Laura and I headed out to food stands near the public beach and ran into Sophie and Joanna. After talking through the rain we went to the beach for a quick dip. There were an assortment of people around, some Australian/Americans (couldn’t figure them out but they had serious cameras and boats), there was also a barbecue going on, and to the side of the barbecue was the fattest pig I’ve ever seen. It was not attached to anything, and didn’t seem to be on the menu, but I of course immediately thought of the scene in “Parks and Rec when Ron brings a pig to a barbecue. But in this clip– Ron brings a stuffed animal compared to what was on this beach. There were dogs barking at it, trying to attack it. But the pig really paid no mind because it could have simply sat on the Lab and it would have been game over for the overconfident dogs.

As we were in the water, Sophie had an idea to go to a random Catamaran that was anchored a short swim away from shore and ask to go on the deck. Now, I found this to be a bit of a strange desire, but I suppose it isn’t a bad idea, but when we started figuring out what to do with our stuff, I felt indifferent enough to stay back and watch our stuff – but mostly to lay in the sand and let the water rush over me. Soon a deep storm cloud arrived, with company of other angry clouds. It was clear that 1) we definitely should not have gone to Tetiaroa, and 2) thunder and lighting are on their way. I think I might have actually seen lightning – which will make my next choices seem a bit dicey – sorry mom! Soon Sophie, Laura, and Joanna are on a small dinghy with a boy coming to pick me up, and what am I supposed to do, say no? So I swim out to the dinghy and jump in face first while introducing myself, because manners are important. It turns out the Caterman that they swam out to had a family from Glendale living in it. You can leave America, but boy does it follow you around. Bryan is 23 years old, just graduated from SF State, and works in Visual Effects with his dad in LA. They’re here for a few weeks renting a boat and sailing around Tahiti and Mo’orea. I soon realize that Bryan is not heading in any particular direction but rather “showing us around” (we have been here for ten weeks I write with an eye roll) – and the storm clouds are looming in my mind and sky. Soon Bryan breaks the dinghies on coral, and he discloses he actually just learnt how to use it. Another moment where I wished Bryan’s frontal cortex had already developed was when he took us back to the boat instead of the shore, when the rain started to come in (all due respect to Bryan he was really trying his best). Bryan’s dad was taking a video of us arriving (maybe they haven’t seen people in a while), and I got to look at the Catamaran briefly before we had to start heading back. We offered to swim back but thankfully Bryan’s mom insisted that he drive us back, but at this point that poor dinghies does not have much power to it– or direction. At this point the wind has picked up so much that the current is rough, the rain is coming in fast and hurts when it hits my skin. Bryan kept on apologizing about how slow it was, to which I tried to be reassuring by responding that we were lucky enough to not concerned with time (I personally was more concerned about the possibility of lightning shooting out of the sky, but tried to remind myself that worrying about it was not going to make any difference). We made it back slowly to the edge of the beach since the current pushed us out. The rain is so heavy we can no longer see any of the boats anchored. We try to give Bryan Sophie's instagram so he can join us at the bar later, but the rain is hitting random buttons on his phone and we have to leave it up to his memory.

We thank Bryan and jump out of the boat and immediately start to run all the way across the newly deserted white sand beach. The rain was harsh and noisy, but our giggling the entire time made it extraordinarily fun. It was also lovely to have adrenaline to help me run faster than I have in a long time. Luckily after a minute or two we saw through the rain that our bikes and bags were still there– but no pig! What happened to it? How did it move so quickly? Add this to the many mysteries of life.

Once we were at our bikes we had a moment of deliberation of what we should do: the storm was not letting up any time soon, we could go back to the food truck, or we could just do the two mile ride back to Gump. We choose the latter and get on our bikes, still in our bikinis. As we were biking with occasional traffic on the one-laned road, I wondered to myself if this was the smartest choice to be riding on moving metal, but again, worrying wasn’t going to make it better. So we just went faster. Luckily, when I got thirsty I could simply just stick my tongue out! Perks of riding through a rainstorm! Once we were back to Gump the rain lightened up and we were in fantastic moods. It turns out his memory was good because at the bar Sophie was trying to convince him to come join us. He had a myriad of reasons why he couldn’t make the journey: the dinghies was broken, it was an expensive taxi ride (we countered with hitchhiking), and that his parents didn’t want him to go. So that is the end of the story with Bryan from Glendale.

We were supposed to go to Tetiaroa on Friday, but that got cancelled too. We had our big feast on Friday, where we made banana pudding, smoked pig (I did not partake in this Gentile activities), and taro. We spent the morning cracking open shells of coconuts, opening them with a macheette in one hand and coconut in the other, and then sitting on a slab of wood with a knife on the end to carve out the coconut flesh(?). Unfortunately in my time here I have learned that I am allergic to coconut, and each time I try to test this allergy it does not go well (hives, throat issues). But I figure that these activities would be fine, but I learned that this too was not within my tolerance. We prepared for our final performance which we have been slowly preparing for over our ten weeks here. We choreographed our dance, and learned lullybis– since that is the way Tahitian babies learn about their culture, we were taught we needed to learn from the basics too. (It was slightly mortifying when we met students from University of French Polynesia (UPF)and before you meet another group formally in Tahitian you sing songs to greet each other, but by that point– we pretty much responded to their beautiful Tahitian song by singing “Itsy Bitsy Spider”).

Today was our last full day in French Polynesia, and a large group of us woke up early early to bike over to the public beach to cheer on Addison who was running the Mo’orea 10K. The beach was full of tents and people cheering the runners on. There is only one main road on the island so people had to run around cars and traffic, but I saw my scuba instructor and we saw a few of the musicians from the bar, it felt nice to be able to recognize people of the Island. We were all jumping and cheering when Addison ran by, clearly the loudest group, and then quickly ran over to the finish line to cheer for a second time. After he finished the race we went into the water for our morning swim. I looked over as I was laying down in the sand trying to soak up the landscape here, the colors, and the air. An impossible task but one I keep trying to achieve. In my line of vision there were little kids dragging each other on a boogie board, giggling when they fell off and standing up only to be covered head to toe in sand.

We had been holding out to go to Tetiaroa on Sunday, the plan was to bring our luggage on the boat, and after they dropped everyone off on Mo’orea we were going to the port in Pappeetee and then go to the airport. I was really looking forward to going from Atoll to Airplane within eight hours, but it was too good to be true. Nonetheless I am looking forward to my last day on the island, and trying to appreciate the fleeting moments left.

I look forward to writing again, for I have more to fill you in on, and this doesn’t feel ready to close out.

More soon,

Rebekah
