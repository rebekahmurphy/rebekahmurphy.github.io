Confrontations with Lemon Sharks the French

Hello friends and family,

I hope you are well. I had a busy weekend, and have a busy week coming up, but I wanted to make sure to write it all down. Our last week includes a trip to Val’s farm, hopefully a chartered boat to Tetarioa Atoll (fingers crossed– major drama!). We are having our final presentations, performance, and preparing a feast– all on top of our homework, readings, etc. All to say, lot’s going on in this part of the Pacific!

This was our last full weekend in Mo’orea– I fly out the 29th of March. Laura, Emerson, Sahana and I all biked the entire island on Saturday (a bit of a last minute decision). It was roughly forty miles and we set off at 7am on our single geared cruisers. We arrived at our first stop after an hour and a half, a public beach – close to where I had done field work with Catie as mentioned in the last post. It was cool to be familiar enough with the island to know roughly where we were, and how far it was from the next familiar place. At the beach there were lagoon boat tours waiting in the water when we arrived, and slowly more and more people emerged onto the beach and into the boats. You may be surprised that almost every single person on the boats were local Polynesians, either from Tahiti or Mo’orea. A lot of second homes on the island are owned by Tahitians who come to Mo’orea for the weekend, and locals on Mo’orea enjoy doing a lot of the tourist activities. Laura, Emerson, and Sahana went to a nearby Motu – but since I had already been to one (humble brag) I felt like I was justified to lay on the beach to have some rest and alone time before the long day ahead and listen to Luke Wallace.

On this beach there was a young couple with their newborn baby, set up with a beach umbrella, ice chest, and a Persian rug instead of a beach towel– very sophisticated! There were two friends who were laying in the sun, three little kids of various ages running past every so often, and two stray dogs laying on the beach, changing locations every so often. There was a group of what I assume were elderly French residents who seemed to be in an organized swimming group doing laps to arbitrary buoys in the lagoon. There was an old guy walking around with his eyes intently on the lagoon, and I wondered who he belonged to, until a similar aged woman walked out of the water and together they walked over to pick up their kayak (after we had a miming conversation where I tried to motion and ask if they needed me to move our bags that were in front of the kayak, to which the lady responded with a shaking head and “french words french words”). Soon they kayaked away from the beach in perfect paddle sync. I looked over to my left at the young French couple who weren’t that much older than I am, maybe thirteen years, and then back at the old couple paddling away, and I saw glimpses into the different stages of life waiting before me, which is constantly existing in plain sight, yet, I had a moment of appreciating how simple and beautiful it is to be a human taking part in the cycle. I suppose spending so much of the last years at a college campus I am not witness to these ordinary cross generational encounters as much. I’m very pleased to be at my current stage of the cycle: laying directly on the sand (although the Persian rug did look really cool), drinking my coca cola, collecting shells, and importantly not having to take turns snorkeling to take care of the little life form I just pushed out. Life is good.

We got back onto our bikes and headed further towards the southern part of the island, which is the most disconnected from the big hotels and clusters of shops that make up a sort of “downtown”. We stopped at a water station to fill up our water bottles, and a family had their trunk parked close to the spigots, and their three little boys got out of the car to begin filling the plastic bottles in the trunk. You can see this a lot over the island, they sometimes serve as third spaces, and people are filling up dozens of empty gallon coca cola bottles. We pressed on, headed towards the restaurant which was my big motivator for going on this bike ride (best Poke!). There were many more Independent flags hanging outside of people's homes on this part of the island, almost all of them had a UN flag flying alongside them, too. The Independence movement is more associated with poorer people, whereas the more well off and higher educated people want to remain a territory of France for the CPP Franc, healthcare, etc. The conversations I’ve had with locals about this is not out of loyalty for France, but more so a disappointed understanding that French Polynesia is not ready for independence yet, but everyone has hoped for independence eventually. Many independent nations in Oceania also have become proponents for Deep Sea Mining out of economic interest, and often have to allow foreign fisheries into their EEZ for the same reasons, despite having policy to be proactive about Climate Change. All of this is very complicated. Would love to talk about it more if you’re interested, but I will stop myself here. All to say, it is very interesting to see these flags on this side of the island– even more so flying alongside the UN flag. Our lunch was delicious and very much worth it. The bike ride took most of the day because of the various stops we would make at different beaches, and made pit stops at a few shops to pick up my last (and first) souvenirs. Once we arrived back at Gump I parked my bike and jumped straight into the bay.

I went to bed promptly at 8:30 to wake up at 6am for my last day of scuba diving. The boat heading out was full, there was a couple from San Diego, two Lithuanians– who I had recognized from the Ferry ride from Tahiti who made conversation with our group– they are here on a three year contract to do construction, but can’t leave French Polynesia. We’ve discussed them at dinner, all interested by the concept of feeling stuck in paradise, because apparently all they want to do is return to Lithuania. There was a young Dutchman who was making his way home from New Zealand. He was heading back to the Netherlands to sell all of his stuff, and go on a quick motorbike ride up to the Baltics as a goodbye to Europe before moving permanently to New Zealand. When we said goodbye I wished him luck in New Zealand and he wished me luck in Amsterdam– he made it clear he was confused why I was going to be in Amsterdam for April instead of California. I enjoy these sorts of conversations as a traveler, and enjoyed going off on my own to do these dives so I could feel like a traveler, even just for the morning before going back to my more "permanent situation” of studying abroad.

The dive itself had encounters that I was not anticipating. The Lemon Sharks were back. We became good friends– I didn’t have much of a choice. I saw maybe double the amount of Lemon Sharks this weekend, well maybe not, but they were much much closer than last weekend, at one point two of them were swimming directly to me, maybe a long arm's length away from my face. I am not a boy so I am not going to lie to you and say that it was “awesome”, I was surely relieved when they turned into a new direction. A few minutes later we saw it eat (or chomp violently quickly) the fish around us. In my enlightened and logical mind I knew I was going to be okay and it had no interest in hurting me, but there is only so much that logical thinking can do when something you’ve been told is dangerous your whole life is swimming right towards you. Besides that, it was cool to see all of the fish and the coral. We saw a few turtles, and at one point I saw one disappear and blend into the coral, only to see a Lemon Shark emerge over the edge. Okay enough with the sharks. I was not that freaked out.

I got my dive certification (woot woot!) and got to say goodbye to my favorite divemaster, Xavier as he drove me back to Gump. After eating lunch I immediately changed into a new bathing suit, threw my clothes back on, and headed out to my bike to get over to the trailhead for our riverhike. A few weeks ago Jennyfer had taken a group out to this spot– which was a locals only spot– but I had woken up just a minute too late to join. I had been bummed ever since, but luckily Emerson remembered the way and so Laura, Sophie, and I followed him blindly– with only one hiccup when we were presented with three different paths (luckily it was the path with the chain). The hike was muddy because it had been raining off and on since lunch. We walked through different pineapple fields and various fruit tree farms, at one point we could hear buzzing from above the trees, and it sounded like there were a thousand bees above our heads, and so we walked quickly so the horror movie that was emerging in my imagination would not actualize. We eventually arrived at the beautiful river where there were perfectly placed rocks, with rushing water going every which way down each crevice. The thick tree canopy meant that we felt a few drops of the rain and only heard the rest. I laid my neck down on a rock to put my head down into a stream that was flowing quickly down a few feet, and felt the rushing water pull my long hair down. It was fun! We walked back through more farm land, and past a sign in French that said “we are watching you thieves”. Our bikes were still behind the bush where we stashed them, and we made our way back, with a quick pit stop for ice cream at the beach, before getting back to Gump in time for dinner. Busy day, good day.

The rain only kept coming, and was coming down on Monday when we toured Val’s farm, where there were dogs chasing chickens so quickly we saw a chicken fly off into the distance. We are working on symposium presentations for class and have been hearing from guest speakers preparing us for Tetiaroa, which has been canceled again and again due to weather, but should be going tomorrow (fingers crossed). We heard from “Dick Bailey” who founded The Brando, and owns other luxury hotels in French Polynesia. We also heard from “Hinano Murphy” an Elder, and her husband Frank Murphy, who is a fellow Murphy Berkeley geography major! We also had a special class visitor, Jean Yeves-Meyers who taught us for a few days during our second week here, and he came to say goodbye. Everyone was so excited we erupted into laughter and clapping when we saw him through the glass doors, and so he threw his hands up into the air. What a boss way to enter a room. A lot of the people clapping I had remembered disliking him, but he was such a character with memorable quotes that we often use, no one could not like him. It was lovely to feel such joy out of something so silly.

Although I am sad about this particular experience of my life coming to an end, I am also excited to bring what I’ve learned about myself and the world back to my everyday life. Last night after dinner Laura, Sahana and I walked over to the bar for a beer and chocolate crepes. We talked about which restaurants to go to when we are back in Berkeley, and what we will miss about being here. We ran back in the rain and I quickly climbed into bed.

A few days ago I got up early and sat in my hammock and saw two dragonflies playing with each other. Today at lunch I watched some spectacular clouds, with two Terns playing underneath them, chasing each other all around the sky. I hope you have had time to witness flying friends underneath the moving clouds in your sky.
