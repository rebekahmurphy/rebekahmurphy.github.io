It's Been Too Long, Here Are My Bullet Points

Hello friends and family!

It’s been too long. I am going to write bullet points of some things I have been doing these last (almost) three weeks to catch us up. Maybe more posts will come soon, no promises though!

  • We only have two weeks left here: Crazy. I don’t feel ready to leave, and I am afraid to tempt fate by returning to regular life (whatever that means). I feel physically much better, just simply being relaxed and living day to day here. It’s a beautiful thing to experience after being so tense– even though last semester was my favorite at Berkeley so far– I only hope to carry the feelings I have here, with me back home.
  • Our last class of the program: Today we had our first data science class, with a couple who have a data consultant firm together, and they seem quite cool. We are using NotebookLM to summarize thirty years of undergraduate field research here at Gump, to assign them OrcidIDs, as a way to let Gump know what research has gone on – it seems there is some accidental separation there. After class I called Maddy and then biked to the Super U, listening to Hannah Cohen’s “Draggin’” . As I got back to Gump, Leah and Julia texted to do the “Valley Loop”, which is eight and a half miles total, and so by the time we got back to Gump it was dark, but it was absolutely beautiful. It turns out there is a paved road into this valley that looked like magic (the valley looks like magic, not the paved road, although that was nice too!). The lighting was just right, so when we walked into a field/lookout point the greenery looked almost dull, illuminating the sun set on the mountains, topped off with a bit of haze. It was spectacular. I was so grateful I did not leave this island without witnessing this beauty. Half of the way back was steep hills riddled with rocks and bumps– definitely not what our beach cruisers are capable of, but on the downhill, we screamed with fear which made it fun!
  • The last two weekends I’ve been scuba diving out of a dive shop in an abandoned hotel. It had been the largest hotel on the island, with plenty of bungalows that sat over the lagoon, but today they sit boarded up, with the decks falling into themselves. Ultra creepy. The dive shop rents a building from the property. They give me rides back to Gump, and so I have had very interesting conversations while they drive along the oceanfront road. My favorite ride was with a scuba instructor who drove me home in his non-street safe car (he specified for France, here it’s fine). He had lived in Algeria, Madagascar, and Thailand before settling down in Mo’orea. He spent twenty minutes talking about his garden– he is getting bees soon in case you were wondering. It is always a treat to learn more about people and their takes on this world. He’s given me rides a few times since, but in the company truck, which is much less fun than the car with wires hanging out everywhere.
  • I went open ocean diving this weekend: As I began my first descent into the open ocean I was immediately faced with a lemon shark coming straight at me. Although I’ve seen reef sharks, I was not exactly ready to see one so up close and personal, you might say. As we sank to the bottom there were about five reef sharks around at any given moment, with a few Lemon Sharks popping in and out. The reef sharks were swimming away from the Lemon Sharks, but you know, so super chill, no big deal, just another Sunday. There were so many fish, and they were so unconcerned by us humans. I was underneath huge schools of fish and grabbing onto coral to watch a sea turtle lay on the ocean floor. At one point a humongous trigger fish began chasing after my instructor, biting his fins. And, by the way, whatever fish you are visualizing in your head – it’s not big enough. Plus it has teeth. The weekend before when I was doing my lagoon dives, we saw (and I counted !!) fourteen eagle rays, all flying together underwater. It was beautiful. Their faces were so distinct, when I was underwater I thought they looked like seals, but with the body of rays.
  • Policy class: When I was taking my hiatus from this blog, I was taking a class with a man named Jean who is an environmental anthropologist who was teaching us Environmental Management and Policy. The content was interesting: temporary or permanent closures in lagoons for conservation purposes, but figuring out a way to uplift fishermen too. Unfortunately he was a very monotone presenter, and we would often have class from 8am-5pm. For the last four weeks we have spent a lot of time behind the computer, and it is interesting how disagreeable it feels after scarcely using it before. My eyes begin to hurt. I feel old! The fieldwork in the class was awesome, though.
  • Walking around the southern part of the island doing fieldwork: We had a UPF student with us (I realize I never wrote about our wonderful exchange with UPF students– will add to the list!), and our task was to collect river water samples, log types of fencing, and investigate side streets we were able to walk down to reach the lagoon. Walking down random dirt roads here was something I wouldn’t have unprompted, but was very happy and comfortable doing so, since we had a purpose. A good lesson for life, no? The day we did this was a holiday for the arrival of Christianity, and so everyone was off. The first side street we went down, a group of maybe ten eight year olds were hanging out, and taking turns riding an electric bike that was far too big for them, giggling down their street. After a few twists and turns we stumbled upon an unspeakably beautiful white sand beach, where there was not a person in sight. I was prepared with a bathing suit (obviously) and walked in for a lovely morning dip. It was a lovely day, and let me just say, we did not find a better place to take a dip afterwards. Good to seize the moment when it is before you.
  • Local fisherman: for a class we fished with a local fisherman named Irwin, and ate the parrotfish he caught while net fishing. His son and grandchildren were always with him, they come from a famous fishing family on the island. He told us that when he was a child he was able to go on week long fishing trips with his parents, and just camp on any beach, but he cannot take his son or grandchildren to do the same because the relationship around property has changed so drastically in his lifetime. The scene on the beach after we watched Irwin fish was awesome– his son made a fantastic fried fish right on the beach, and there was a group of women de-scaling fish, and they were kind enough to offer them to me and my friend Maya, so I can say I’ve had fresh fish right off the bone of the ocean!
  • Kayaking fieldwork: When my partner and I were dropped off it was only ten minutes in to our journey when the waves started crashing into our kayak and the thunder and lightning ensued. We looked around for where we could beach – but the first option before us had very effective guard dogs that looked hungry and angry. No thank you! Luckily soon there was a small cement ramp where we pulled up to, and the man grabbed us two chairs so we could sit in his backyard. So very kind. I had never heard thunder clap so close to me. (That week there were periods of heavy rain, lightning and thunder that would wake me up. Turns out I revert back to being a little kid, reminding me of when we would climb into our parents bed to pull the covers over our heads to protect ourselves from the storm. Pulling the sheets over my head in the middle of the South Pacific in my twin bed at 5am does not have the same effect, the more you know!) Once he started opening his windows back up we knew that it was probably safe to get back on the kayak and resume our journey. The new Harry Styles album just came out and so we listened to that as we recorded the armoring of the coast and conducted our transect sampling. I was with Catie, a yoga instructor, sorority bird-nerd from Wyoming– we had a good time. We eventually met up with the other kayak group, meaning we were done with our work. We kayaked to a nearby motu (islands within the lagoon), beached our kayaks underneath three palm trees, and dipped in the crystal blue waters. The brush inland was thick, and made me think it was probably what much of Mo’orea looked like before development and ten thousand people arrived not so long ago (mostly Polynesians from Society Islands, European expats are apparently more recent). We kayaked back across the channel to the shore and waited for Jean who was an hour and a half late, except that was no problem because we were laying on the most idyllic beach I had never allowed myself to dream about, letting the water rise to my back and back down again. I have never been so happy for a tardy French man before.

    • When he did eventually arrive, we began trying to fit three kayaks into the back of a pickup truck, which were all halfway out of the bed, tied together with a thin rope, with boy scout knots. Seven of us piled into the car, with three up front and four in the back. We were all having a grand time, Evan connected to the bluetooth and started playing “Whole Wide World” by Cage the Elephant We all cheered when we heard the line about Tahiti. I was extremely surprised by our professor Jean being totally chill, even posing for an obnoxious selfie people were taking. I would have understood if we had to remain completely silent the whole ride back, but realized when we got back to Gump it wouldn’t have made a difference, we were fine and never got pulled over. A good lesson because instead of a tension filled drive back, it became a fun story to tell.

  • Ferry troubles led to hitchhiking adventure: Over this Saturday the class went to Tahiti for a market tour, and a hike. I woke up at 6am with everyone, knowing I’d have to leave Tahiti before lunch and the hike, but excited to see the market we saw after our flight when we first arrived, in action. However, just as the 8:00am ferry was leaving the port I found out that the ferry I would have to take to make it to my scuba session was leaving at 9:15 from Tahiti. So, we barely had left the ferry dock in Papeete before I turned around and went back on the very ferry I had just arrived on. Oh well, I didn’t feel very bitter about it because I still got to be in the hub-bub of the Saturday morning ferry chaos. For example, I usually sit up on the deck for the fresh air and the views, but for the last few ferry’s I sit with my friend Laura down in the “cafe section” of the boat. I notice people who regularly take this ferry sit in the middle– furthest away from the views– but so they can sit at a table and talk to each other. Often, people come over to greet a friend, and spend the time catching up. This boat ride back I noticed since it is Spring Break Time, I noticed a significant amount of tourists compared to February, when it was almost all locals riding the ferries. Anyways back to my story (or my morning, depending on how interesting this is to you), I luckily had brought my companion, “The Human Condition” by Arendt and went about my business. Part of the reason I wasn’t too bothered that I went all that way for nothing was that I knew I could hitchhike back. An adventure in itself. It only took about five minutes or so until a car pulled over. It was a grandma driving, with her daughter in the back holding her three year old daughter. The exact car you want to be picked up by. The kindness of strangers is perhaps my favorite part of traveling – they pointed out to me where they lived, yet continued to drive an extra ten minutes past to drop me off right at Gump. I had been prepared to walk part of the way, knowing that it was a far distance from the station, but they gave me princess treatment to the max.
  • Tourists: I have taken to the habit of doing many ordinary tasks outside, for example brushing my teeth or hair outside. A few times now, I wake up and go outside to brush my teeth and I see a tour bus outside of Gump with a dozen or so retired Americans (with their adventure outfits on!) being told about Gump station. Very interesting. Even more so, they never notice me. Genuinely: one time, I walked over to the large group who had conveniently decided to do the debriefing to the side of my hammock, and they didn’t bat an eye! Every few days a cruise ship enters the bay, and I wonder if they are dumping poop into the bay, yet I swim anyway. The tourists are very interesting– not to say I am not one– but I see rows of eight ATVs going down the road. Why do they need ATVs on a paved road? I find it really interesting the outdoors correlation with the tourist industry, like when I see a Texan wearing their brand new Chacos at the Rijks museum.

Clearly this was more than bullet points, but I am happy to get my thoughts and expeirences out. I am truly so sad about leaving soon, trying to soak everything in, the feelings and the place. So grateful to be here. So grateful for life. Sending my well wishes to you as always.

Talk soon!

Rebekah
