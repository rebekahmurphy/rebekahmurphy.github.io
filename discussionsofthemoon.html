Tuesday, Feburary 3, 2025. (Should have been posted Sunday, Feburary 1st, 2026, apologies)

Hello friends and family,

I am writing to you from a couch in the living room. It is a vibrant space: a large game of banana grams in one corner, a group of three playing hearts at the table. The rest of us are either reading on the surrounding couches or working on our computers. Sounds of Dylan and the Velvet Underground off of someone’s playlist, intense chatter and the sound of the sink with someone being responsible and doing dishes. A slight breeze pouring through the open windows with shell beads as curtains. I realize I have not spoken much about the people on the program. Many nights include card games that make it feel like the sleepaway summer camp I never experienced, and days off are spent tanning and swimming by the pool and in the lagoon. There is a large variety of majors among the twenty of us here: econ, data science, conservation resource studies, mcb, urban studies, plant biology, etc. The group is quite lighthearted and fun, but also have been able to have really interesting long conversations with people. It is fun to hear about people's adventures throughout the world and the experiences we have all had, and talk about experiences yet to come. Classes will depend on which professor is teaching us for the two weeks, but it is all very relaxed (so far).

It is now Monday, and I have been struggling to find the time to sit down and write this. Not necessarily because I am so busy, but because reading, being in the water, and being with people spontaneously is more enticing than sitting on my computer. There is much to cover and I don’t think I have that in me, we will see what happens.

Details that don’t necessarily have a place within any storyline but are details I don’t want to forget, include: walking up to the shore after snorkeling to find friendly local neighbors offering us delicious fresh mango and shellfish (sorry Hashem) drenched in lime that they had just caught, driving back from Miss Mo’orea at 11pm illuminated by the full moon as Sophie tries to teach Emerson how to ask to hit someone's vape in French, successfully hitchhiking for the first time and immediately getting picked up by a Coral Gardeners van on their way to a birthday party, floating in the lagoon, and watching Mo’orean teenagers on their way back from school dancing on the ferry deck. There is free time sprinkled throughout the day, never long enough to sit down and write, but enough time to read and play games with everyone.

This last week we spent much of it hiking with a Terrestrial Ecologist named Jean-Yves . He was a fantastic character, and so very French. Such a character that whenever his name is brought up along with a phrase he said off-handedly, it is almost guaranteed to make the room erupt into giggles. He took us on a four hour hike, which felt like a long strenuous hike, to the peak of one of the many peaks on the island, however it turned out to only be a mile. This is because he would stop every few hundred steps to explain to us another important plant that we needed to know. It was incredible knowledge and such a unique experience, however he was not worried about making sure everyone was paying attention/and or in ear shot, and so many of his explanations were only heard to the few that were at the front of our line. Once I am able to upload pictures with better wifi, I will share not only our hikes but also his particular taste in footwear. However, he was a conservationist through and through, and was so much so that he was disinterested in humans, and wished that he could see the environment before humans arrived. This made me wonder about the origins of conservation through John Muir, and how he thought the land he was seeing was in its “natural state”, even though we know that Yosemite was heavily farmed and curated by the Native Americans. I wonder if striving for the “original nature” is a zero sum game, since humans have always changed “original nature”, and in terms of Mo’orea, how far back are you willing to go before humans are satisfied? But then again we run into the earth timescale vs human timescale. Were we ever meant to see the “original-nature”, or are we highly invasive for a reason?

We went to Tahiti last Friday and visited a Mosquito lab , largely funded by the French government and various wealthy people who like to come to French Polynesia but are bothered by the mosquitos (I say with a slight eyeroll). Interestingly the lab is in close communication with other mosquito labs in Singapore and China. Since they are all funded by their governments the exchanging of ideas is much more open, compared to the few privatized American companies who are starting up, to which the director described their work as a “black box”. These labs sterilize male misquotes– who don’t bite and thus don’t spread any desises– and release them to then sterilize the females who are spreading diseases. Although DDT almost eradicated misquotes in the seventies, as we know from Joni Mitchell, put away that DDT, we want spots on our apples!

Over the weekend we went to Miss Mo’orea, apparently beauty pageants are a very big deal in French Polynesia. It was at the island sports complex, and it was so unusual for me, but also so much fun. The crowd consisted of the four contestants' family members and friends, and it was clear that the competition was between contestants two and four, since the majority of the crowd would erupt in cheering whenever they were on stage. We all got dressed up and thoroughly enjoyed it. We were the only non-Polynesians there, and I was very grateful for our program that we are able to see these important cultural events even if it is not science related.

We have begun our next class with Chris Meyers who is such an expert that he can talk for hours (which he does), but it is almost always a pleasure to try and follow along with an expert's thought process (except in an evening lecture). This is our last two weeks at our beach house until we move to Gump and it feels quite freaky how quickly time is moving. This experience feels like a unique opportunity to see myself without any stressors of normal life and just be, as well as not having to worry about logistics while I’m here– different from another kind of trip. I wrote in my journal a few days ago that I have many moments of peace here, but that I don’t entirely feel at peace, which I think is good considering my age. There is much to wonder about with the future, the person I will continue to become, and the opportunities that will unfold or fold within the near future. Being completely at peace I think would take away from all that is to come. Maybe peace isn’t the right word, maybe I mean contentment. Who knows. My takeaways so far is that I feel much healthier waking up at 6:30 and going to bed at 10pm, moving throughout the day, but also that my curiosity has not been dispelled. I was hoping after last semester where it felt like I was trying to grapple with the big ideas (insert the horrors of attempting to map without progress), I would enjoy learning about the small details of island biogeography and ecosystems.

But the big questions still persist, like “what would happen to our perspective on science if we didn’t always use the Enlightenment way of thinking” and the following thought is “how standardized we’ve all become as a consequence, but also to our benefit.” I’ve also wondered why we use terms like “territorial” for an animal like a fish, which is associated with land and property, and why we should project our human behavior onto a fish. Chris countered with the important fact that we are made up of chemicals just like fish are too, and so why not? Like last semester I become annoyed by myself with some of these questions since they are wildly unhelpful and counter productive. I find myself doubting everything we humans think we have “figured out”-- and then I wonder why my attention isn’t going towards the way that animals have evolved to create such symmetry with one another? I just find the big ideas so much more fun, and dissatisfied with answers that follow. But in order to ask these big questions I ought to understand the ideas that I am attempting to critique, and in that vein, we all see how wildly out of my depth I am.

The sun has been a constant companion since we last spoke. I’ve been enjoying going out right before dusk to just sit and watch the water until dinner is ready. I appreciate the rewards that comes after being still for so long, and the way that fish or different animals poke through crevices and the water. The clouds are also joyous to watch, they cover the mountain peaks on some days, other days you can see every detail of vegetation. Without the dark looming cloud cover I feel much more like I am on an island in the middle of the South Pacific. Light always makes the surroundings change throughout the day no matter where you are, but here it feels especially poignant. The water changes clarity and color throughout the day, as well as the illumination of the mountain peaks. I enjoy sitting and listening to the waves crash and appreciate how quiet it can be here. Emerson and I went on an evening stroll the other night and we both gasped at the erupting pink moon peaking through low clouds, where it was touching the water. As the night progressed it rose in the sky and once again became the familiar small white full moon. Neither of us had our phones and so we just had to sit with the fact that there was no way to capture it. Plus, I don’t think my iphone camera does anything justice on this island.

I think I will leave this here. As always I hope that you are well and have moments to appreciate the moon (looking at you, mom), and I look forward to talking soon. More to come.

Rebekah
